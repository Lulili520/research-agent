# Paper impact prioritization

Use this guide when the user asks for influential, seminal, landmark, representative, or high-impact papers. Impact determines discovery and reading priority; it does not determine whether a paper's claims are correct.

## Evidence dimensions

Collect dated, source-attributed signals where available:

1. **Scholarly uptake**: citation count from a named index, citation velocity or year-normalized citations, and influential-citation indicators when the platform defines them. Never combine counts from different indexes as if they were identical.
2. **Venue recognition**: best/distinguished/test-of-time paper awards or official spotlight/oral distinctions. Verify against the conference or professional society rather than snippets or author biographies.
3. **Intellectual influence**: later papers explicitly build on, extend, challenge, or establish a research line from the work. Support this through citation-context inspection, surveys, or multiple later papers—not citation count alone.
4. **Practical adoption**: incorporation into widely used systems, standards, benchmarks, datasets, libraries, models, or products. Record concrete adoption evidence and distinguish official use from repository mentions.
5. **Artifact traction**: code/model/data availability, independent reproductions, downstream integrations, and repository activity. Stars and downloads are supporting signals only and are time- and platform-dependent.
6. **Durability and breadth**: influence across years, venues, institutions, tasks, or adjacent subfields rather than a brief burst within one cluster.

## Time-aware selection

Divide eligible papers into cohorts before ranking:

- **Foundational/established**: old enough for durable uptake to be observable.
- **Recent influential**: has meaningful normalized uptake, adoption, or recognition despite a shorter exposure window.
- **Emerging/high-potential**: too recent for mature citation evidence but has strong direct relevance plus early independent adoption, official recognition, or follow-on work.

Compare papers primarily within the same age and subtopic cohort. State the publication cutoff and the date each dynamic metric was observed.

## Decision method

Use a transparent evidence profile, not a universal weighted score. For every shortlisted paper:

- record each available signal, source, observation date, and uncertainty;
- label the impact basis as established, recent, emerging, or unclear;
- require at least two independent signal families for an unqualified `high-impact` label;
- allow one exceptionally strong signal only with explicit wording such as `highly cited` or `official award winner`, not the broader `high-impact` label;
- preserve technically central or contrasting papers even when their popularity is lower.

If the user needs a strict shortlist, use a configurable rubric and disclose it. A reasonable default is to select a balanced set rather than a single leaderboard:

- 30–40% foundational/established papers;
- 30–40% recent influential papers;
- 20–30% emerging or technically essential papers.

Adapt these bands to the field's age and requested time range. Do not force a cohort that has no credible candidates.

## Disallowed shortcuts

- Do not use CCF category, venue acceptance, raw citation count, author fame, repository stars, or search-result rank as a standalone impact score.
- Do not compare raw citation counts across publication ages or substantially different subfields without normalization and caveats.
- Do not treat survey citations, self-citations, negative citations, or perfunctory mentions as equivalent to method adoption when context matters.
- Do not omit relevant newer work merely because it has had less time to accumulate citations.
- Do not use impact prioritization to replace paper-level validity and reproducibility appraisal.
